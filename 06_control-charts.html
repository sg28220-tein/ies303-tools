How the limits were obtained

Charts

Reading the pair. The mean chart is sensitive to a shift in the process centre; the range chart is sensitive to a change in spread. A process can pass one and fail the other, so both are read together before declaring the process in control.

Reading the chart

  1. Is any sample outside its limits? Name the sample and say which limit it broke.
  2. Which chart raised it? A signal on the mean chart alone points to the process centre moving. A signal on the range chart points to the spread changing — often a different cause and a different fix.
  3. Do the points inside the limits look random? Drift, a run on one side, or a saw-tooth pattern is a signal even when nothing crosses a limit. The pattern check above tests exactly that.
  4. In control is not the same as acceptable. If the process is stable, is it centred where you want it, and is the spread small enough for the specification?
  5. What would you check on the line before calling this an assignable cause — the operator, the material batch, the measuring device, the machine setting?

How the limits were obtained

Reading the chart

  1. Which samples, if any, sit outside the limits? With counted data one bad sample can be a genuine signal or an unusually large batch — which is it here?
  2. What does a lower limit of zero mean? When the computed LCL is negative it is reported as 0, so the chart cannot flag a process that suddenly looks too good. Is that a problem worth knowing about?
  3. Is there a trend or a step even inside the limits — the last few samples all above the centre line, or a jump partway through the series?
  4. Would a different α change your answer? Re-set the limits by α risk instead of z and see whether the same points still signal.

Does the tie rule change the answer?

Every convention applied to the same data. Ties are common in count data and rare in measurements, so this matters most on p and c charts.

The same two errors as areas under the curve

Both curves are the sampling distribution of the mean, not the process itself, so their spread is the standard error σ/√n. The limits stay where the target put them.

How the indexes were obtained

Why 1.33 and not 1.00. These indexes assume the process stays centred. Real processes drift as tools wear and material batches change. At Cp = 1.00 a typical ±1.5σ drift alone produces about 6.7% defective, because the specification limit sits exactly at 3σ with no margin. At 1.33 the same drift leaves roughly 0.6%.
